## 1. Patients: recall, comprehension, health literacy, prescriptions, language, adherence, follow-up

### Takeaway
Most of what is said in a consultation does not survive the walk out of the clinic (40-80% forgotten, and about half of what is "remembered" is wrong), and in India this lands on a population where roughly 6 in 10 adults in the available studies have limited health literacy, about half of chronic-disease patients do not take medicines as prescribed, and about half of newly registered hypertension patients do not come back.

### Cited Findings

**Recall after consultation (global)**
- "40-80% of medical information provided by healthcare practitioners is forgotten immediately"; "almost half of the information that is remembered is incorrect"; the more information given, the lower the proportion recalled (Kessels, J R Soc Med 2003; narrative review, not a single-sample study). **[F]** — [Kessels 2003, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC539473/)
- Same review: with spoken instructions only 14% of information was remembered correctly, versus over 80% when pictographs were used; written information is better remembered and improves adherence; recall falls moderately with age; low-education patients benefit most from visual formats. **[F]** — [Kessels 2003, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC539473/)

**Health literacy (India; small single-site studies, no national survey found)**
- One Indian study: 60.4% low health literacy, 29.4% average, 10.2% high; even among post-graduates 57.8% had low health literacy. **[S]** (URL-to-figure mapping unconfirmed; one of) — [PMC3714819](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3714819) / [PMC3879879](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3879879)
- Bangalore study: 65.5% limited health literacy (35% inadequate + 30.5% problematic); 34.5% sufficient. **[S]** — [RGUHS National Journal of Public Health, Jan 2025 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/d9e0fa2487db42e0bdc5bf6f411eb8df) (mapping unconfirmed)
- Gujarat, 393 TB patients: 67.1% limited health literacy, worse with lower education and unemployment. **[S]** — [Indian Journal of Community Medicine, Apr 2024 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/d9287fcb00e24f848d14ac43e1dc7b71) (mapping unconfirmed)
- Another outpatient-clinic study found inadequate health literacy in 38% of adults. **[S]** — [PMC3714819](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3714819) (mapping unconfirmed)

**Prescription legibility and comprehension (India)**
- Tertiary centre audit, Karnataka (AIMS, BG Nagar): 11% of prescriptions illegible and 20% barely legible. **[S]** — [Int J Res Med Sci audit](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/1978)
- Another audit: 68.5% easily legible, 20% difficult, 11.5% completely illegible. **[S]** — [Journal of Pure and Applied Microbiology abstract](https://microbiologyjournal.org/abstract-15-4-14/) (mapping unconfirmed)
- Rural Maharashtra tertiary hospital (Australasian Medical Journal, 2011): 17.6% of prescriptions illegible. **[S]** — cited in [Open Magazine, "A Doctors' Malady"](https://openthemagazine.com/feature/a-doctors-malady) (secondary source)
- KEM Hospital, Mumbai: only 12.4% of patients had good overall knowledge of their prescription; 52% ranged from "no knowledge to little knowledge". **[S]** — [IJCMPH: Patients' knowledge of prescribed medication, Mumbai](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/view/5450)
- Separate Indian study: only 50.3% knew the correct dose and frequency; 16.3% of illiterate vs 5.6% of literate patients, and 19.4% of those over 45 vs 5.8% under 45, had not fully understood the prescription. **[S]** — [Patients' Insight of Interpreting Prescriptions and Drug Labels, PMC3670850](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3670850/) (mapping unconfirmed; may be the JCDR 2016 paper at [jcdr.net](https://www.jcdr.net/articles/PDF/7462/14579_CE(Ra1)_F(GH)_PF1(SSAK)_PFA(AK)_PF2(PAG).pdf))

**Language barriers (India)**
- India has 22+ major languages while biomedicine runs in English; internal migration is increasing language-discordant consultations; the issue "has received very little attention in India". **[S]** — [Narayan, "Addressing language barriers to healthcare in India", Natl Med J India](https://nmji.in/nmji/archives/Volume-26/Issue-4/SFM-II.pdf)
- Qualitative Indian study found six effects of language discordance: frustration, lack of rapport, trust issues, patient dissatisfaction, compliance problems and patient-safety threats. **[S]** — [Impact of Language Barriers in Doctor-Patient Relationship, PMC9842973](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9842973/)

**Medication non-adherence**
- WHO (2003, "Adherence to Long-Term Therapies: Evidence for Action"): in developed countries adherence in chronic disease averages only 50%, and is lower in developing countries; hypertension adherence 27% in Gambia, 43% in China, 51% in the USA. **[S]** — [WHO news release, 1 July 2003](https://www.who.int/news/item/01-07-2003-failure-to-take-prescribed-medicine-for-chronic-diseases-is-a-massive-world-wide-problem)
- India, type 2 diabetes: systematic review/meta-analysis of 16 studies; pooled non-adherence 48% among MMAS-8 studies; range 11%-68% across studies; predictors include older age, polypharmacy, treatment complexity, psychological distress, access barriers. **[S]** (fetch blocked by CAPTCHA) — [PMC12914141](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12914141/)
- India, hypertension: meta-analysis of 40 studies, 18,808 patients; pooled non-adherence 48%; rural 57%, community settings 51%, eastern India 76%. **[S]** — [Current Hypertension Reviews (Bentham)](https://www.eurekaselect.com/article/150468)
- CONFLICT: a second India meta-analysis (12 studies, 3,164 participants, 2025) reports pooled *adherence* of only 15.8% (95% CI 4.4-43.4), i.e. about 84% non-adherent; very wide CI. **[F]** — [Hipertensión y Riesgo Vascular 2025](https://elsevier.es/en-revista-hipertension-riesgo-vascular-67-articulo-prevalence-antihypertensive-medication-adherence-associated-S188918372400117X). The 48% figure rests on the larger evidence base and is the safer one to quote.

**Missed follow-up / loss to follow-up (India chronic disease)**
- India Hypertension Control Initiative: of 21,895 patients registered Jan 2018-Jun 2019 at 24 sentinel facilities, only 11,274 (51%) returned for a follow-up visit in Jul-Sep 2019. Among returners BP control rose from 26.3% to 59.8%. **[S]** — [J Clin Hypertens, Apr 2021 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/841e8782a6f64d9099dfbefd1173f513)
- Mumbai Hypertension Project (private clinics in urban slums; J Hum Hypertens 2022; n = 13,184 enrolled Jan 2019-Feb 2020): 48.7% never returned for even one follow-up. BP control among those who followed up was 29-43% vs 14-22% across all enrolled. A successful follow-up phone contact was associated with returning (aOR 2.76); drug vouchers aOR 3.61. **[F]** — [PMC9510164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9510164)
- Later IHCI analysis: nearly one-fourth of patients had no follow-up visit in the previous 12 months. **[S]** — [PubMed 38199804](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38199804/) (mapping unconfirmed)

### Inferences
- The patient-side failure is not mainly "lack of access to a doctor" but loss of the consultation's content afterwards: spoken, too fast, in an unfamiliar language/script, on a prescription up to ~30% of which is hard or impossible to read.
- Kessels' pictograph result (14% -> 80%+) and the Mumbai phone-contact result (aOR 2.76) are the two findings that most directly justify a feature: structured, visual, written, vernacular instructions plus an active outbound follow-up contact.
- Follow-up loss (about 50%) and non-adherence (about 48%) are similar in size, so a reminder-only feature addresses half the problem; the other half is the patient never re-entering the loop.

### Gaps
- No nationally representative health-literacy survey for India was found; all figures are single-site studies with small samples. NFHS-5 was not checked for a usable proxy.
- No India-specific measurement of post-consultation recall (an Indian equivalent of Kessels) was found.
- No quantified India figure for the share of consultations that are language-discordant.
- Several India figures above are [S] with unconfirmed URL mapping.

## 2. Doctors: consultation length, documentation load, burnout, missing records, doctor-patient ratio

### Takeaway
An Indian primary-care consultation averages about 2 minutes against 20+ minutes in Sweden, so there is no slack for explanation, history-taking or documentation; roughly half of Indian residents meet burnout criteria; the headline national doctor ratio (1:811) hides counted AYUSH practitioners and a steep rural shortfall.

### Cited Findings

**Consultation length**
- Irving et al., BMJ Open 2017: 179 studies from 111 publications, 28,570,712 consultations, 67 countries. Mean length ranges from 48 seconds (Bangladesh) to 22.5 minutes (Sweden). 18 countries holding about 50% of the world's population have consultations of 5 minutes or less. Length is associated with physician density, health spending, admissions for ambulatory-sensitive conditions, and physician satisfaction. **[S]** (BMJ and Southampton pages returned 403) — [BMJ Open 2017;7:e017902](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/7/10/e017902); record at [Edge Hill University](https://research.edgehill.ac.uk/en/publications/international-variations-in-primary-care-physician-consultation-t/)
- India's figure in that review: 2 minutes (2015 data point). **[S]** — [Medical Dialogues coverage](https://medicaldialogues.in/doctors-in-india-see-patients-for-just-two-minutes-study)
- Indian government super-speciality OPDs: gastroenterology, neurology and oncology clinics saw 200+ patients per working day with an average of two minutes per consultation. **[S]** — [IJCMPH: Patient-doctor ratio across nine super speciality clinics](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/view/5276)
- Press reports of one doctor handling 300-400 OPD patients a day against a norm of about 40. **[S]**, news/blog quality — [DailyRounds](https://www.dailyrounds.org/blog/?p=3088)

**Documentation and admin time (US data; no Indian time-motion study found)**
- Sinsky et al., Ann Intern Med 2016;165(11):753-760: 57 physicians, 16 practices, 4 US states, 430+ observed hours. 49.2% of office time on EHR and desk work vs 27% on direct face time; nearly 2 hours of EHR/desk work per hour of patient contact, plus 1-2 hours of after-hours work per self-report diaries. **[S]** — [The Hospitalist summary](https://the-hospitalist.org/?p=407); [HC Innovation Group](https://www.hcinnovationgroup.com/clinical-it/news/13027410/study-ambulatory-physicians-spend-half-their-time-on-ehrs-desk-work)
- Medscape 2024 Physician Burnout & Depression Report (9,226 US physicians, 29+ specialties): 62% cite bureaucratic tasks (charting, paperwork) as the leading cause of burnout. **[S]** — [Becker's](https://www.beckersphysicianleadership.com/physician-workforce/why-physicians-are-burnt-out.html)

**Burnout (India)**
- Meta-analysis of 10 studies, 3,062 Indian postgraduate residents: pooled burnout prevalence 50.9%. **[S]** — [Int J Med Students](https://ijms.pitt.edu/IJMS/article/download/4727/3399/45609)
- Nationwide survey of 763 Indian healthcare practitioners: personal burnout 47%, work-related 31%, patient-related 35%; 24.9% had all three. **[S]** — [Manipal research portal](https://researcher.manipal.edu/en/publications/assessment-of-burnout-among-healthcare-practitioners-in-india-a-s/)
- Narrative review: 30-75% in medical students, 60-80% in residents (up to 85% in surgical specialties), 35-65% in practising physicians. **[S]** — [Int J Res Med Sci](https://www.msjonline.org/index.php/ijrms/article/view/17176)
- IMA study (reported 2017, early findings): over 75% of doctors have faced violence at work; relatives/attendants responsible in 68% of cases; 46.3% name fear of violence as their main stressor; 62.8% cannot see patients without fear. **[S]**, sample size not retrieved — [DailyRounds report of IMA study](https://www.dailyrounds.org/blog/more-than-75-doctors-have-faced-violence-while-performing-duty/); background review at [PMC7500628](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500628)

**Missing prior records**
- Smith et al., JAMA 2005 (Colorado, USA): 253 clinicians, 1,614 visits; clinical information was missing in 13.6% of visits, most often lab results, letters and history. **[S]** — [AHRQ PSNet](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/missing-clinical-information-during-primary-care-visits)
- ABDM analysis: teleconsultation records "are not consistently retrieved at subsequent in-person encounters"; cross-facility retrieval "remain[s] far below the thresholds needed to generate clinical value". **[F]** — [HMPI, July 2026](https://hmpi.org/2026/07/09/from-infrastructure-to-impact-operationalizing-indias-ayushman-bharat-digital-mission/)

**Doctor-population ratio**
- Government statement to Lok Sabha (MoS Health Anupriya Patel; reported 2025): 13,86,157 registered allopathic doctors and 7,51,768 AYUSH practitioners; assuming 80% availability of both, the ratio is 1:811, "better than the WHO standard of 1:1000". **[S]** — [ETV Bharat](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/!health/doctor-population-ratio-in-india-is-estimated-to-be-1-811-centre-enn25080105862); [Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/health/doctors/indias-doctor-population-ratio-1811-govt-tells-parliament-159970)
- Counter-view: 1:1,000 nationally, "dropping to 1:4,500 in rural areas". **[F]** (stated in the article without a primary citation visible to me) — [HMPI, July 2026](https://hmpi.org/2026/07/09/from-infrastructure-to-impact-operationalizing-indias-ayushman-bharat-digital-mission/)

### Inferences
- With 2 minutes per patient, any feature that asks the doctor to type more will be rejected; HMPI says exactly this about ABDM ("can add time rather than save it"). A feature must remove work from the consultation (history collected before, explanation delivered after).
- The 1:811 figure only works by counting AYUSH practitioners and assuming 80% are active; quote it with that caveat.
- Violence and burnout data link the doctor's problem to the patient's: relatives are the aggressors in 68% of cases, which points to unmet information needs of families, not only security.

### Gaps
- No Indian time-motion study of documentation/admin share of doctor time was found; the 49% figure is US ambulatory practice with EHRs and should not be presented as Indian.
- No quantified estimate (India or elsewhere) of minutes lost re-taking history because records are unavailable; only the 13.6% US "missing information" proxy.
- Medscape's overall burnout percentage for 2024/2025 was not retrieved; no 2025 or 2026 edition figures were found.
- Irving's India figure is a single 2015 data point from the review; I could not open the full text to check how many Indian studies underlie it.

## 3. Administrators: no-shows, waiting times, operational problems

### Takeaway
Globally about 1 in 4 outpatient appointments is a no-show; Indian evidence is thin but points the same way, with forgetting and absent reminders as the top reasons. Reported Indian OPD waits range from about 12 to 60 minutes for a roughly 2-minute consultation, and registration-counter queues are the least-satisfying step.

### Cited Findings

**No-shows**
- Dantas et al. systematic review (105 studies): average no-show rate about 23%; highest in Africa (43.0%), lowest in Oceania (13.2%); main determinants are long lead time and prior no-show history. **[S]** — [reference record](https://www.textbookofdigitalhealth.com/references/dantas2018noshows.html)
- USA: missed and unfilled appointments cost about $150 billion a year; about $200 per unused 60-minute slot; no-show rates 5-30%. This is an industry (SCI Solutions) estimate, not peer-reviewed. **[S]** — [Healthcare Finance News](https://www.healthcarefinancenews.com/news/missed-appointments-cost-providers-150-billion-annually-report-says)
- England: over 15 million GP appointments (about 5%) wasted each year, costing £216m. **[S]** — [NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/images/textmarketer/website/missed-gp-appointments-costing-nhs-millions/)
- India, tertiary-care hospital outpatient study: forgetfulness (mean 4.05 on a 5-point scale) and lack of appointment reminders (mean 4.00) were the leading reasons for non-attendance; respondents were 66% urban, 61% aged 25-40. **[S]**, sample size and no-show rate not retrieved (page 403) — [Health Science Reports, Jul 2024 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/a1a6a19a568949a5a453351f60fd1e22)
- India vendor claim: median private-hospital no-show rate 32% (25% to 40% range); a clinic with 1,200 monthly appointments loses 384 slots and over ₹3 lakh a month. Vendor marketing blog with no disclosed method; use only as an illustration. **[S]** — [caller.digital blog](https://caller.digital/blog/hospital-no-show-reduction-india-sms-vs-voice-ai)

**OPD waiting times (India, single-hospital studies)**
- Maharashtra tertiary hospital: mean wait 59.0 ± 39.5 minutes. Tamil Nadu tertiary hospital: mean total wait about 32 minutes (00:32:25 ± 00:18:17). Gujarat tertiary hospital: 12.16 ± 2.35 minutes. Shorter waits were significantly associated with higher satisfaction; patients were least satisfied with waiting at the registration counter. **[S]**, URL-to-figure mapping unconfirmed — IJCMPH papers: [3232](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/download/3232/2369/13529), [11281](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/download/11281/6835/51826), [11781](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/view/11781), [12592](https://www.ijcmph.com/index.php/ijcmph/article/download/12592/7635/59820)
- One of those studies: mean satisfaction score 68.13%, dissatisfaction 20.87%; long waits and poor privacy were the main complaints. **[S]** — same IJCMPH set (mapping unconfirmed)
- CAG audit of Uttar Pradesh hospitals has a chapter on outpatient services (registration load, consultation time); I did not open it. — [CAG Report No. 2 of 2019, Chapter 2](https://cag.gov.in/uploads/download_audit_report/2019/Chapter_2_Out_patient_Services_of_Report_No_2_of_2019_Hospital_Management_in_Uttar_Pradesh_Government_of_Uttar_Pradesh.pdf)

**Doctor verification / credential trust**
- WHO report based on 2001 Census data: 57% of those claiming to be allopathic doctors had no medical qualification; 31% were educated only to secondary level; in rural areas only 18.8% of allopathic doctors had a medical qualification. The government first called the report erroneous and later accepted it. Data are 25 years old. **[S]** — [Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/57-percent-fake-doctors-in-india-who); [Gaon Connection](https://gaonconnection.com/?p=26390)
- IMA estimate: more than 10 lakh quacks in India, about 50,000 in Delhi. **[S]**, association estimate — [The Wire](https://thewire.in/health/what-is-delhi-doing-about-its-unlicensed-medical-practitioners); see also [ORF, "Medical impersonation and the appearance of legitimacy"](https://orfonline.org/research/medical-impersonation-and-the-appearance-of-legitimacy)

**Queue/scheduling at scale**
- ABDM "Scan and Share" OPD registration: 22.7 million tokens issued in Uttar Pradesh, showing that registration-queue digitisation is the part of ABDM that has scaled. **[F]** — [HMPI, July 2026](https://hmpi.org/2026/07/09/from-infrastructure-to-impact-operationalizing-indias-ayushman-bharat-digital-mission/)

### Inferences
- The two top Indian no-show reasons (forgot, no reminder) are cheap to fix, but vendors themselves report that standard reminders rarely get below about 20%, so reminders alone are not an innovation.
- An admin's unused slot and a patient's 30-60 minute wait are the same scheduling failure seen from two sides; a feature that reallocates predicted no-show slots or gives live queue position serves both.
- Doctor verification is a real trust problem in India, but the best-known statistic is from 2001 census data; present it as historical.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, multi-site Indian no-show rate was found, and no Indian cost-of-no-show estimate other than a vendor blog.
- No survey of Indian clinic managers/administrators about their operational problems (queue, scheduling, complaints handling) was found; the admin role has the weakest evidence base of the three.
- No data on complaint/grievance volumes or resolution times in Indian outpatient settings.
- No MGMA figures were retrieved.

## 4. Hand-offs: the loop after the visit

### Takeaway
After the visit the loop routinely stays open: between 7% and 62% of outpatient lab results are not followed up, only about a third of referrals end in a documented completed appointment, and patients recall about half of their medication list even when handed a summary. Teach-back and written summaries help understanding and are well liked, but evidence for hard outcomes is mixed.

### Cited Findings
- Callen et al., J Gen Intern Med (systematic review, 19 studies, ambulatory settings): failure to follow up lab results ranged from 6.8% to 62%; imaging from 1.0% to 35.7% (imaging range from the published abstract as I recall it; only the lab range was in my search results); consequences included missed cancer diagnoses. **[S]** — [AHRQ PSNet](https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/failure-follow-test-results-ambulatory-patients-systematic-review); [UNSW newsroom](https://newsroom.unsw.edu.au/news/health/doctors-failing-follow-test-results)
- Patel et al., J Gen Intern Med 2018 (large US health system): of 103,737 referral scheduling attempts, only 36,072 (34.8%) resulted in a documented completed appointment; 38.9% of attempts had no appointment date recorded. **[S]** — [PubMed 29532299](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29532299/); [PMC5910374](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5910374/)
- A "50% of specialty referrals are never completed" figure circulates (attributed to a 2017 study); the origin could not be traced to a primary source. **[S]**, weak — [WellSky blog](https://stagewellskytest.wellsky.com/blog/2021/10/21/the-future-of-referral-systems-closing-the-loop)
- After-visit summaries, RCT (Pavlik et al., J Am Board Fam Med 2014): medication recall accuracy was 53% at 2 days and 52% at 3 weeks, with no difference between AVS content versions. **[S]** — [JABFM 27(2):209](https://www.jabfm.org/content/27/2/209.long)
- After-visit summaries, survey of 355 patients (J Patient Experience 2020): 82.8% recalled receiving one, 67.4% consulted it, 31.6% shared it with someone; of those recalling personalised free-text instructions, 96% found them easy to understand and 94.4% useful. **[S]** — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2374373519879286)
- Teach-back, systematic review (Yen and Leasure, Fed Pract 2019): AHRQ and IHI recommend teach-back as a universal health-literacy precaution; results for readmission were mixed, with 2 of 6 studies showing statistically significant improvement; one quality-improvement project using AHRQ's toolkit reported a 45% drop in mean 30-day readmission rate. **[S]** — [PMC6590951](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6590951)
- India: outbound contact closes the loop. In the Mumbai Hypertension Project a successful follow-up phone call was associated with returning for follow-up (aOR 2.76); BP control was about twice as high in those who returned. **[F]** — [PMC9510164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9510164)
- India: the ABDM consent loop itself fails to close in busy clinics: "patients are frequently unaware that a notification has been sent". **[F]** — [HMPI, July 2026](https://hmpi.org/2026/07/09/from-infrastructure-to-impact-operationalizing-indias-ayushman-bharat-digital-mission/)

### Inferences
- A static summary is not enough (recall stays near 50% whatever the layout); the gain comes from personalised instructions and from checking understanding. This supports an interactive after-visit step (patient confirms back what they will do) rather than a PDF.
- Nearly a third of patients share the summary with someone else, which supports a caregiver-facing view.
- None of the retrieved studies measures the reverse flow, i.e. whether the doctor learns the outcome. That absence is itself a finding the team can state as an unmet need, but it must be worded as "not measured", not as a statistic.

### Gaps
- All quantitative hand-off studies found are from the USA/Australia; no Indian data on test-result follow-up failure, referral completion or follow-up booking rates.
- No study quantifying how often doctors receive outcome feedback after outpatient treatment.
- No Joint Commission material was retrieved. The teach-back review was read only as a search summary; one figure in it ("59% vs 44% at 12 months, P = .005") was ambiguous as to direction and outcome, so it is omitted.
- The imaging range for Callen is from memory, not from a retrieved page.

## 5. What current digital health apps and ABDM leave unsolved

### Takeaway
India has enrolled people at enormous scale (93+ crore ABHA IDs, 100+ crore linked records by mid-2026), but independent analyses say clinical use of those records between facilities remains very low, the workflow costs doctors time, and older, rural, less literate and non-English users are largely outside the system.

### Cited Findings
- Scale: over 104 crore health records linked to over 93 crore ABHA accounts (government figures, mid-2026); accounts grew 14.7 crore (2021) -> 30.4 (2022) -> 50.6 (2023) -> 72.2 (2024) -> 84.5 (2025) -> 90+ (2026). **[S]** — [News On AIR, 22 May 2026](https://newsonair.gov.in/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-achieves-milestone-of-over-100-crore-health-records-linked-with-abha/); [PIB document, July 2026](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/jul/doc202676912801.pdf)
- HMPI (Kommuru and Agarwal, Stanford GSB, accepted July 2026): 880 million IDs, 500,000+ facilities, 860,000+ professionals; yet "active record linkage and cross-facility retrieval remain far below the thresholds needed to generate clinical value"; milestone M3 (actual record exchange) "remains extremely low"; Rajasthan ABHA-to-EHR linkage "below 20%"; engagement "plateaus once field teams withdraw". **[F]** — [HMPI](https://hmpi.org/2026/07/09/from-infrastructure-to-impact-operationalizing-indias-ayushman-bharat-digital-mission/)
- Same source, doctor workflow: the steps required "during a two-to-five-minute consultation in a high-volume public facility can add time rather than save it". Legacy hospital systems lack ABDM-compliant APIs; integration cost for a mid-sized provider is estimated at INR 30-50 lakh. Labs and LMIS vendors are stuck in a "circular incentive gap". **[F]** — [HMPI](https://hmpi.org/2026/07/09/from-infrastructure-to-impact-operationalizing-indias-ayushman-bharat-digital-mission/)
- Delphi study of Indian community-medicine experts (Indian J Community Med 2023; 17 then 15 panellists): 73% strongly agreed ABDM could add to the workload of existing staff; unanimous that under-use by marginalised groups and human-resource constraints are major challenges; other barriers were awareness, device/internet gaps, staff digital literacy, data security. **[F]** — [PMC10263032](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10263032)
- Policy commentary: digital delivery disadvantages "senior citizens, women, rural population and illiterate people"; state and central systems are not interoperable. **[S]** — [IMPRI policy update](https://www.impriindia.com/insights/policy-update/ayushman-bharat-digital-mission-abdm-2020/)
- Preprint (single author, not peer reviewed): 65% of government facilities vs 35% of private hospitals participate in ABDM (Aug 2025). Low-confidence source. **[F]** — [Zenodo, Feb 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/18594090)
- Older users: 41% of Indian elders own a smartphone, 13% use the internet or social media, and 5% use online services such as banking or health apps (HelpAge-type survey figures); another analysis puts online participation of the 60+ at 9.4%; smartphone access falls from 57.9% at ages 50-59 to 44.7% at 60-69 and 42% at 70+. **[S]**, mapping across these sources unconfirmed — [Deccan Herald on HelpAge survey](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/india%2Fkarnataka%2Fbengaluru%2F88-seniors-offline-helpage-urges-action-3585484); [NCAER IHDS Research Digest, July 2026](https://ncaer.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IHDS-Research-Digest-July-2026.pdf); [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/opinion/from-aadhaar-to-upi-how-indias-digital-rails-can-include-not-exclude-older-adults/3053392/)
- Rural older adults (DAHLIA study, Geriatrics 2022): digital literacy 11%; health literacy 3-27% across domains; 50% own a mobile phone but very few a smartphone; under 10% used the internet to contact a health professional; barriers were poor literacy and poor vision. **[S]** — [Geriatrics, Mar 2022 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/84c3b157657d43428b43f94bc238b9f1)
- Apps: systematic review of mHealth adoption in India concludes there is little evidence that mHealth has improved outcomes in India at scale. **[S]** (CAPTCHA blocked fetch) — [PMC11998589](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11998589/)
- Apps: JMIR 2025 review of heart-disease apps available in India found negligible Indian-language coverage and low engagement; more than 70% of the health apps had no reviews or ratings. **[S]** — [JMIR 2025;27:e53823](https://jmir.org/article/export/ris/jmir_v27i1e53823)
- Apps generally: dropout and attrition are the dominant problem; reminders, feedback and human/peer support improve retention. **[S]** — [PMC9092233](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9092233/)

### Inferences
- The unsolved problem is not record storage or booking (both exist at national scale) but getting a record used at the next encounter and getting information back to the patient in a form they can act on.
- Because 59% of elders lack a smartphone and about 90% are not online, a patient-only app misses the highest-need users; access through a family member's account, SMS/voice and regional-language output are the realistic channels.
- MediBridge's "Care Score" and reminders sit in the crowded part of the market (engagement features with known attrition); the differentiator has to be on the hand-off.

### Gaps
- Government M3 (record-exchange) numbers were not found; the "very low" characterisation rests on one practitioner journal article.
- The private-sector participation split comes only from a non-peer-reviewed preprint.
- No head-to-head evaluation of Indian consumer apps (Practo, Eka Care, mfine, etc.) on comprehension, adherence or follow-up outcomes was found.
- Connectivity statistics for rural clinics were not retrieved.

## 6. Caregiver and family involvement in India

### Takeaway
Family is the default care system for India's older adults, yet LASI-based analyses show that only about a quarter of elders with functional limitations actually get family help and about 10% have no primary caregiver; no study quantifying how often relatives manage an elder's medicines or appointments was found.

### Cited Findings
- LASI-based IIPS analysis: 48% of older adults have at least one functional limitation, and only 23% of them receive help with activities of daily living from family; spouses remain the main carers even when children co-reside; women are more likely to receive care. **[S]** — [IIPS, "Rethinking family care"](https://www.iipsindia.ac.in/sites/default/files/Rethinking_family_care_0.pdf); [IIPS, "The Caregiving Dilemma"](https://iipsindia.ac.in/node/10113)
- Nearly 10% of elderly Indians lack any primary caregiver as co-residence declines. **[S]** — [IIPS, "Evolving Family Dynamics"](https://iipsindia.ac.in/node/10667)
- Indian caregiver-burden study: 67% of caregivers had mild burden scores (below 20); burden was highest in the "inadequate caregiving skills" and behavioural-disturbance domains and rose with weekly hours and lack of family support. **[S]** — [Journal of the Indian Academy of Geriatrics, Sep 2024 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/208bbbfa90894515ae2777774c6394bd) (mapping unconfirmed)
- Qualitative work on hospitalised older adults: family caregivers face practical difficulties at admission, during treatment and at discharge, with financial stress and isolation. **[S]** — [PMC12738501](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12738501)
- Indirect indicators of family mediation: 31.6% of patients shared their after-visit summary with someone else (US); relatives/attendants are involved in 68% of violence incidents against Indian doctors. **[S]** — [SAGE, J Patient Experience](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2374373519879286); [DailyRounds on IMA study](https://www.dailyrounds.org/blog/more-than-75-doctors-have-faced-violence-while-performing-duty/)

### Inferences
- With most elders offline and family the assumed carer, the family member is the real "user" for a large share of elderly patients, but current app designs model one patient = one account.
- The burden domain "inadequate caregiving skills" suggests caregivers lack exactly the information the doctor gave in the consultation.

### Gaps
- No Indian figure for the share of elderly patients whose medication or appointments are managed by a relative, or accompanied to OPD visits. This is a clear evidence gap, not a known number.
- LASI Wave 1 report itself was not opened; figures are from IIPS summaries.
